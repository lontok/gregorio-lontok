A family history, checked against the record

Gregorio B. Lontok

Lolo, agronomist of the Negros cane fields, 1894 to 1959

He grew up in Batangas, studied agriculture at Los Baños, and spent the late 1920s running sugarcane trials on the haciendas of Negros. He ended his career as Superintendent of La Granja Experiment Station in La Carlota. This page tests the family's own account of his life against old journals, directories, and indexes, and it links every claim to its source.

How to read the labels

  • ConfirmedA source outside the family papers says it.
  • PartialA source supports part of it or fits it closely.
  • Family onlySo far it appears only in the family's own documents.

Family only doesn't mean wrong. It means nobody has found the paper trail yet.

SummaryWhat the record shows

Gregorio B. Lontok was a real, well-documented Filipino agronomist, and most of what the family wrote about his career holds up. College records show he studied at the University of the Philippines College of Agriculture in Los Baños and earned his Bachelor of Agriculture on April 5, 1920 (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 8, no. 7, 1920). Sugar industry journals from 1926 to 1931 name him again and again as a field experimenter at Binalbagan, La Carlota, and Ma-ao in Negros Occidental (Sugar News, 1927). A 1934 alumni roster puts him in Sara-Ajuy, Iloilo, as a hacienda technical adviser (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 23, 1934-35). A 1957 convention list names him "Superintendent, La Granja Experiment Station, Bureau of Plant Industry" (PHILSUTECH Proceedings, 1957). The U.S. Social Security index gives his birth as December 24, 1894, in Batangas, and a Find a Grave index entry agrees (SortedByName, Lontok). A Find a Grave index entry says he died on February 22, 1959, in Bacolod, but no civil death record has been found yet (Ancestry, Find a Grave index). The parts that don't check out yet are the numbers the family recorded, such as yield gains and station income. The wartime cassava story also rests on family memory alone. Eight of the nineteen items on his list of writings can be matched to printed sources. The mistakes on that list look like typing slips, not invented work.

A Batangas boy trained at Los Baños in 1917 to 1920

The family says Lolo was born in Taysan, Batangas. His college records list his home as Rosario, Batangas, which looks like a conflict at first (UP Commencement, 1920). It isn't one. A 1903 law folded Taysan into Rosario, and Taysan only became its own town again by an executive order of November 11, 1918 (LGU Rosario, Batangas, History). Through all of his school years, a clerk would have written "Rosario" even for a Taysan boy. No baptismal or birth record has turned up, so Taysan itself stays at Partial. The U.S. Social Security death file, as transcribed online, gives his birth as December 24, 1894, in Batangas. It names his parents as Doroteo Lontok and Maria "Barbaera," a spelling that may be a transcription error (SortedByName, Lontok).

The earliest trace of him at Los Baños is a fungus specimen he collected there in December 1917, now in a USDA collection under "Lontok, Gregorio" (USDA ARS Biocollections, BPI 770534). In November 1918 the college printed an honor roll, and his name is on it. It lists students "who responded to the call of their Country on October 10, 1918, for service in connection with the World War" (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 7, no. 4, Nov. 1918). A December 1919 review of the college's rice research says that "a piece of work has been started, under the writer, by Gregorio Lontoc" on which traits of rice varieties actually vary (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 8, no. 5, Dec. 1919). That was his senior research under Nemesio B. Mendiola.

His degrees need a little explaining. In 1919 the college offered two tracks. A six-year course for intermediate-school graduates led to the Bachelor of Agriculture, and a four-year course for high school graduates led to the Bachelor of Science in Agriculture (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 8, no. 5, Dec. 1919). The university later ruled that B.Agr. holders couldn't become master's candidates. So the college created a two-year supplementary course in history, English, and electives, and finishing it "raised" the B.Agr. to a B.S.A. (Hester, The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 12, no. 10, Mar. 1924). Lolo took that path. The college's own directory of graduates lists him under the Class of 1920, B.Agr., and again under the Class of 1923, "Bachelor of Science in Agriculture (Supplementary)" (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. XI, 1922-23). A June 1924 thesis abstract signed "Abstract by Gregorio B. Lontok" carries a footnote saying it was "required theme work in English 101," which fits that supplementary English requirement (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 13, no. 1, June 1924).

The family also says he finished every M.S.A. requirement except the thesis. The supplementary B.S.A. existed to open the door to a master's, so the claim is plausible. But no record of master's work turned up in 275 digitized issues of The Philippine Agriculturist, so it stays Family only (Internet Archive, The Philippine Agriculturist collection).

While finishing his second degree he already had a job. Around 1922-23 the college directory lists him as "In Charge, Citrus Project, United States Department of Agriculture, Los Baños, Laguna" (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. XI). USDA plant inventories record a Philippine woody climber "presented by G. B. Lontok" and received May 24, 1923, and a second item sent through J. E. Higgins of Los Baños in November 1924 (USDA Inventory).

A caution for anyone searching on their own: two other Lontoks were at the same college at the same time. Ambrosio M. Lontok of Lobo graduated in the same 1920 class (UP Commencement, 1920). Mariano Lontok was a student in 1918 (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 7, no. 4, Nov. 1918). The "G. B. Lontok Memorial Elementary School" in Lipa honors a different man, and "G. B. Lontok & Co." in Manila was a trading firm with no known link to him (Laws and Resolutions, 1957, Philippine Yearbook, 1951, p. 123).

He tested the new canes from Java, 1926 to 1931

In the 1920s a virus called mosaic disease was damaging the old sugarcane varieties in cane countries around the world. In Puerto Rico, where mosaic was first seen in 1915, it put the industry "on the verge of destruction" until mosaic-resistant varieties replaced the old ones (Garcia et al. 1957). The best known of the resistant canes came from Java, and a 2021 history of Java sugar calls P.O.J. 2878 a "wonder cane" (Widyasari et al. 2021, Sugar Tech). Philippine planters had to find out, farm by farm, whether it would beat their local Negros Purple. Lolo was one of the field men who ran those tests.

He worked for the Philippine Sugar Association (PSA), which ran a shared experiment program with the sugar mills, called centrals, and their planters. In 1926 the PSA listed him as "Assistant in Cane Investigation, Binalbagan Estate," and he sat on its committee on cane varieties, diseases, and fertilizers (PSA Compilation of Committee Reports, 1926, Proceedings of the PSA Annual Conference, 1926). College directories of 1927 and 1928 call him "Assistant Pathologist, Binalbagan Sugar Estate" (The Philippine Agriculturist, 1927). A 1927 Sugar News item describes him as "assistant in experimental work for La Carlota, Binalbagan, and Ma-ao," which matches the family's claim almost word for word (Sugar News, 1927). By 1928 he was listed at La Carlota (Sugar News, 1928).

His own words survive from this time. He wrote a letter to Sugar News dated December 16, 1927. In it he praised La Carlota planter Luis Jalandoni, who "produced an average of 139 piculs of sugar per hectare." He added, "Last year when I was brought to this place by Mr. Pritchett, the then Director of Experimental Work, I discovered that Mr. Jalandoni already was enthusiastic over experiments" (Sugar News, vol. IX, 1928). Sugar News remembered that letter 25 years later in a 1953 look-back column (Sugar News, 1953).

The experiments themselves show up in print with his name on them. A 1929 trial run jointly by Oscar Ledesma of Hacienda Salamanca, Central La Carlota, and the PSA carries the caption "P. O. J. 2878 Outyields all Other Varieties" and the line "Experiment harvested by Gregorio Lontok" (Sugar News, 1929). A January 1930 report adds that "Negros Purple has in every case been outyielded" (Sugar News, 1930). Another 1930 trial at Hacienda Casalagan, "layed out by Gregorio B. Lontok" with twenty replications, found that stripping dead leaves from the cane gave no extra yield (Sugar News, Jan. 1930). In a variety trial the same year, he and Mr. Lopez reported that New Guinea 24-A "must be harvested late if good yields should be obtained" (Sugar News, 1930). The PSA's 1931 annual report credits "LONTOK" on trials at Haciendas Salamanca and Consuelo (PSA Annual Report, 1931).

His best-known paper is "Fertilizer Experiments at Binalbagan," Sugar News vol. IX, pp. 170-172, March 1928 (Sugar News, vol. IX, 1928). It tested whether Negros silt loam needed phosphorus and potash on top of the nitrogen planters already used. The USDA, Chemical Abstracts, and the 1932 congress of the International Society of Sugar Cane Technologists in Puerto Rico all indexed it (USDA Agronomy Current Literature, 1928, Chemical Abstracts, 1929, ISSCT Proceedings, 1932).

The family document goes further than the record in two places. It says his report "Fertilizers and Fertilizer Practices for La Carlota Sugar Central District" stayed in use. It also says he was credited with raising La Carlota district yields quickly from 1924 to 1931. No copy of the report and no source crediting him with a district-wide yield increase has been found, so both stay Family only (Google Books search notes, Lontok).

Sara-Ajuy and the missing decade, 1932 to 1947

The family says he moved to Sara-Ajuy, Iloilo, from 1932 to 1937. The record agrees on the place. A 1933 Sugar News item reports that "Mr. Lontok, Administrator of Hacienda Serruco in the Sara-Ajuy District, has finished his first year of operation of that hacienda with a crop of 11,110 piculs of sugar averaging 96 piculs of sugar per hectare" (Sugar News, 1933). The volume's own index lists "Lontok, Gregorio 198," which points to this page, so the item is Confirmed. The 1934 college silver anniversary roster is clearer: "Gregorio B. Lontok, Technical Adviser, Hacienda Ynchausti & Co., Sara-Ajuy, Iloilo" (The Philippine Agriculturist, vol. 23, 1934-35). The Ynchausti firm had incorporated the Central Azucarera de Sara-Ajuy around 1928 (Wikipedia, Sara, Iloilo).

The family's claim that he "doubled the district's yield" can't be tested. The 96 piculs per hectare figure is one hacienda's first-year crop, not a district figure. The Wikipedia summary shows mill output rising in 1929 to 1931, before his documented arrival, so the rise can't be credited to him from these sources (Wikipedia, Sara, Iloilo). That claim stays Family only.

After 1934 the paper trail goes quiet until 1949. None of the sources checked name him between 1938 and 1947, which includes the war years (Google Books search notes, Lontok).

The war years rest on family memory alone

The family story says he lived in Manapla during World War II, that his children went to Manapla Elementary School, and that the family evacuated to Hacienda Corazon in Victorias. It says he planted 16 hectares of cassava during a famine in 1943-44, and that people from Cadiz, Manapla, Victorias, Saravia, and Silay lived on that cassava for three months. No record found names him in wartime Negros, so each of these details stays Family only.

What the record does show is that the setting is accurate. The Japanese landed on Negros on May 21, 1942, and families rushed to the mountains while prices rose and hoarding spread (Hofileña, SunStar Bacolod, July 13, 2021). A study of one Manapla planter's wartime papers shows guerrillas and Japanese soldiers both taking rice and livestock from civilians around Manapla and Victorias. It also shows that one guerrilla command, the North Negros Sector, covered "Silay-Victorias... and Cadiz-Manapla" (J. D. Hofileña, Journal of Southeast Asian Studies, 1996). That may be why the family story names this exact cluster of towns. Saravia is the old name of today's Enrique B. Magalona, and all five towns lie within about 25 km of each other (PhilAtlas, Enrique B. Magalona, PhilAtlas, Manapla). A Victorias local history says residents fled "to the mountains and neighboring haciendas" and that food "was rationed" (A Pinoy in Korea, Victorias during WWII).

A Hacienda Corazon does exist in Victorias City today, but no prewar or wartime record of it has been found (Facebook, HDA. Corazon Youth Organization). Severe food scarcity in these years is well documented. But no source found uses the word "famine" for northern Negros Occidental in 1943-44, so that claim is Partial (Hofileña, SunStar Bacolod, July 20, 2021). Cassava was the right crop for the moment. A review calls it "the drought, war and famine crop" because it yields on poor soil with little input (Burns et al., Sustainability, 2010). A trained agronomist planting it on hacienda land fits everything we know. The scale, 16 hectares feeding five towns for three months, is something no source found can check.

Superintendent of La Granja in his last years, 1948 to 1959

La Granja was the station he came back to. It began as the Spanish "Granja Modelo" at La Carlota. Sources give founding dates from an 1881 royal decree to "about 1890" (La Carlota City, History, Sugar News, 1930). War disrupted it in 1899, and the Bureau of Agriculture revived it in 1903 (Librero, 1988). In the 1920s it lent PSA researchers staff and laboratories, so Lolo knew the place from his La Carlota years (Quirino, History of the Philippine Sugar Industry, 1974). By 1947 to 1950 it was the Bureau of Plant Industry's La Granja Sugarcane Experiment Station. Staff there bred the Alunan cane and also studied vegetables and fruits (Report of the Secretary of Agriculture and Natural Resources, 1950). Earlier heads included José Sánchez in the Spanish era, Silvestre Asuncion in 1922, F. D. Maramba in 1925, and Paul A. Villar as acting superintendent in 1928 (Revista agrícola de Filipinas, 1908, Philippine Agricultural Review, 1922, Bureau of Agriculture Annual Report, 1925, Sugar News, 1928).

The 1949 Official Directory lists "Gregorio B. Lontok" on the Bureau of Plant Industry pages near the La Granja entry, and also near a column of provincial agricultural supervisors (Official Directory, 1949). The scanned columns are scrambled, so we can't tell which title belongs to him. That fits the family's account of a 1948 arrangement, in which the province paid him as Assistant Provincial Agriculturist while the bureau used him as Acting Superintendent. But the directory doesn't prove it, so the 1948 arrangement stays Family only and the 1949 posting is Partial. A 1951 yearbook lists his address as Bacolod City (Philippine Yearbook, 1951). He was a member of the Soil Science Society of the Philippines from 1950 to 1955 (Journal of the SSSP, 1950, 1955).

He kept writing for farmers in these years. The USDA's Bibliography of Agriculture records "The corn plant and its culture" in Agricultural and Industrial Life, February 1954, and a March 1955 article titled "Keep the soil where it is. Provide it with a continuous free supply of organic matter and return it to near virgin fertility" (Bibliography of Agriculture, 1954, Bibliography of Agriculture, 1955).

The 1957 Philippine Sugar Technologists convention lists attendee no. 36 as "GREGORIO LONTOK, Superintendent, La Granja Experiment Station, Bureau of Plant Industry" (PHILSUTECH Proceedings, 1957). That confirms the title Confirmed. The family gives an appointment date of February 15, 1957, and station income of P41,702.68 for 1955-56 and P60,588.91 for 1957-58. None of these has an outside source yet, so they stay Family only.

The Find a Grave index gives his dates as December 24, 1894, to February 22, 1959, with burial in Bacolod, Negros Occidental (Ancestry, Find a Grave index). The birth date matches the Social Security file, though the two indexes may share an origin (SortedByName, Lontok). The death date comes only from the Find a Grave index, which volunteers build, and the memorial page itself couldn't be opened. So his death in Bacolod on February 22, 1959, is likely but Partial until a civil or church record confirms it. If the date is right, he served as superintendent for about two years. The family biography gives his age as 64, which would date it to 1958 or early 1959. The Social Security file also shows that a claim was received on September 14, 1970, at an office coded "VARO APO928 SANFRAN CA" (SortedByName, Lontok). That code may point to a U.S. Veterans Administration office serving the Philippines, but no service record has been found to explain it.

His writings, corrected and annotated

The family's list of writings reads like a CV he kept himself. Where the items can be checked, the titles and page numbers mostly hold up. The errors are volume numbers and one page range reused from the next item, which look like typing slips. That makes the unverified Spanish circulars more believable too. Several of them restate results he published or harvested in Sugar News in 1929 and 1930.

Two corrections matter most. Sugar News for 1927 is volume VIII, not VII, because the same scan contains the "Index to Volume VIII 1927" (Sugar News, 1927). And the mosaic article isn't at vol. IX, pp. 56-58. It's in vol. VIII (1927) at pp. 997-998. The pp. 56-58 range belongs to his letter to the editor (Sugar News, 1927).

Family leads that still need the family's help

Two family details came up in the research, and neither is confirmed. They're open questions for the family, not facts.

The first is his wife. One user-submitted Ancestry family tree, the Libuit Family Tree, lists an "Esperanza Odjar" with "Spouse: Gregorio B Lontok (1894-xxxx)" (Ancestry family trees, Odjar). That tree cites no sources. "Odjar" may be a variant or misspelling of "Odejar," but no record, index, or newspaper found confirms the marriage under either spelling. Does anyone in the family know Lola's full maiden name, and is there a marriage certificate or church record at home?

The second is a possible son. Sugar industry sources from 1961 to 1965 name a "Gregorio O. Lontok, Jr." who worked as a junior agronomist for the Philippine Sugar Institute in the Ma-ao and La Carlota districts (Sugar News, 1961, Bibliography of Agriculture, 1963). A 1954 college bulletin lists a "Lontok, Jr., La Granja, Negros Occidental" (UPCA Monthly Bulletin, 1954). In Filipino naming, the middle initial usually comes from the mother's maiden name, so an "O." would fit an Odejar mother. But that's only a naming custom. No source states that this man was Lolo's son, and nothing confirms what the "O." stands for.

Open questions and where to look next

These are the places most likely to fill the gaps, for any family member who wants to keep digging.

  1. FamilySearch's Philippine civil registration and Catholic records need a free login. They could hold his 1894 baptism in Taysan or Rosario, a marriage record in Negros Occidental or Iloilo, and his 1959 death record in Bacolod.
  2. The Find a Grave memorial page itself may show the cemetery name, a photo, or linked family members. Only the index row has been seen so far (Ancestry, Find a Grave index).
  3. The 1952 "Bibliography of the Published Contributions ... College of Agriculture 1909-1950" mentions "Lontok" on pp. 22 and 183, which may list his undergraduate thesis (Google Books). The UPLB library could check it in print.
  4. The 1923 and 1924 UP commencement programs aren't online. They would give the exact date his B.S.A. was conferred.
  5. Bureau of Plant Industry annual reports for 1955 to 1959 and the 1959 Official Directory are the best bets for his appointment date and the station income figures (Official Directory, 1959).
  6. For the war years, the Jose Gaston papers from Manapla, the Negros Occidental Historical Commission oral histories, and the 1953 Historical Data Papers for Manapla and Victorias at the National Library are the likeliest places to find his name or Hacienda Corazon.
  7. The 1970 Social Security claim filed through a Veterans Administration office code suggests a U.S. veterans file might exist. PVAO guerrilla rosters and the U.S. National Archives Philippine Archives Collection are worth a search.
  8. Family papers at home are the most direct source for the La Carlota fertilizer report, the 1930-31 Spanish circulars, and the 1950s newspaper articles, none of which are digitized anywhere we could search.

Conclusion

The family remembered him accurately. Where outside records exist, they back up where he worked, what he studied, and the title he held at the end. The gaps sit in the numbers and in the war years. Those are the parts that would have lived in private papers and in the memory of people who were there. That makes the family's own documents the most valuable remaining source, and they're worth scanning and keeping together.

The record also adds to the family story. It shows a student who answered the 1918 call for war service and a young field man whose 1928 fertilizer paper was indexed by a world sugar congress. It also shows an agronomist who spent the late 1920s helping show Negros planters that a new cane from Java outyielded their old one. Near the end of his career he was still writing plain advice for farmers about keeping soil in place and feeding it organic matter. This draft is open to corrections from anyone in the family who knows more.

How this was researched: in September 2026, research agents searched digitized journals and directories on Internet Archive and Google Books, plus genealogy indexes, with Firecrawl and Tavily. They also searched the Consensus and Elicit scholarly databases. Those two had no records of him, which is expected for 1920s trade-journal work, but they supplied the scientific background on mosaic disease, P.O.J. 2878, and cassava. A separate pass then re-opened the key sources to check the quotations and labels.
